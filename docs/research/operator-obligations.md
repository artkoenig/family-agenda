## Short answer

For the Family's data in an Instance the operator is, on the available guidance, a **processor (Art. 28 GDPR)**. It needs an AVV, a sub-processor list, records of processing (Art. 30(2)), appropriate security (Art. 32), breach notice to the customer (Art. 33(2)) and deletion or return at the end (Art. 28(3)(g)). For its own customer, billing and website data it is a **controller** (Datenschutzerklärung, Art. 13). As a seller to consumers it also needs an Impressum (§ 5 DDG), terms (AGB), the withdrawal notice and, new since 19 June 2026, a **withdrawal function** (§ 356a BGB), the **Kündigungsbutton** (§ 312k BGB), and renewal terms within § 309 Nr. 9 BGB. As an Einzelunternehmen it needs a Gewerbeanmeldung (§ 14 GewO) and tax registration (§ 138 AO), with VAT either charged or waived under § 19 UStG, and it is personally liable without limit.

**"Nothing blocks a one-person operation" still holds.** Nothing read creates a headcount, capital, licence or registration threshold that a sole trader cannot meet. Four things raise the cost or change the design, and are the questions for the lawyer:

1. **DPO.** § 38(1) sentence 2 BDSG requires a Datenschutzbeauftragter regardless of headcount if the processing is subject to a DPIA under Art. 35. On the WP248 criteria a DPIA is probably indicated for processing children's data and school-mail content with an LLM (section 9.2), so this is not a side question.
2. **Key custody.** With the operator running the volume, ADR-0006's master key does not protect Family data from the operator (section 3).
3. **EU Data Act switching rules** may apply to the promised export, with no micro-enterprise carve-out in the switching chapter (section 6.4).
4. **Merchant of Record.** Paddle's terms make Paddle the seller of record, but the Supplier keeps the service, support and cancellation duties (section 6.6).

## 1. Role under GDPR

### 1.1 Processor, in the usual reading

- **[read]** Art. 28(10) GDPR: "if a processor infringes this Regulation by determining the purposes and means of processing, the processor shall be considered to be a controller in respect of that processing." So the operator must not use Family data or message text for its own purposes: no content analytics, no model training, no product telemetry derived from content. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj
- **[read]** EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor, version 2.1 (20 September 2022): https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2023-10/EDPB_guidelines_202007_controllerprocessor_final_en.pdf . They contain **no** discussion of the household exemption or of providers serving private users (a text search for "household" and "provide the means" finds nothing).

### 1.2 The household exemption and the operator's role (authority guidance found)

- **[read]** Art. 2(2)(c): the Regulation does not apply to processing "by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity".
- **[read]** Recital 18: "However, this Regulation applies to controllers or processors which provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." So the Family may be outside the GDPR, the operator is not.
- **[read] Authority guidance.** The Landesbeauftragte für Datenschutz und Informationsfreiheit NRW publishes the AUDITOR criteria catalogue for cloud-service certification (version 1.0, 5 June 2024): https://www.ldi.nrw.de/system/files/media/document/file/kriterienkatalog_v1.0_de_final_pub.pdf . It addresses exactly this case. Key sentences (translated from German): the provider keeps its processor duties either way ("Während die Anwendbarkeit der Haushaltsausnahme die Verpflichtungen des Cloud-Anbieters als Auftragsverarbeiter nicht berührt, unterliegt der Cloud-Nutzer nicht den Verpflichtungen der Datenschutz-Grundverordnung"). The duties of Art. 28(3)(a), (b), (c), (d) and (h) are addressed to the processor and stay unchanged. For (e), (f) and (g), which depend on a controller's duties, "there is no point of attachment" ("keinen Anknüpfungspunkt") when the household exemption applies. The catalogue also says a consumer does not automatically fall under the exemption, and that cloud providers in practice do not check, but build a service that is fully GDPR-compliant without relying on the exemption.
- **Reading for Family Agenda:** act as a processor and meet Art. 28(3) in full, including (e) to (g), by contract. For (g) this costs nothing and § 327p(3) BGB (section 6.2) demands the same return of content anyway. Caveats: AUDITOR is a certification catalogue by one state authority, not an EDPB or DSK opinion; it assumes a cloud service, and a hosted Instance for one Family may be closer to a managed application. **[unverified]** The catalogue cites CJEU C-101/01 (Lindqvist), C-212/13 (Ryneš) and C-25/17 (Jehovan todistajat) for a narrow reading of the exemption; those judgments were not read. If a customer is not a purely private household (a small business, a class parent group), the exemption does not apply and the Family is a controller, so the terms can limit the offer to private households.
- **Still open for the lawyer:** whether the operator is also a controller for some processing (Art. 28(10)), and what the AVV says about third parties' rights (4.2).

### 1.4 Operator as controller for its own data

Customer account, contact, billing and website data serve the operator's own purposes: it is controller (Art. 4(7), **[unverified]** wording not re-read). A **Datenschutzerklärung** with the Art. 13 content is needed. **[unverified]** § 25 TDDDG (consent for non-essential cookies) was not re-read; a site without trackers avoids it.

## 2. Records, breaches, liability

- **[read]** Art. 30(2): "Each processor ... shall maintain a record of all categories of processing activities carried out on behalf of a controller". Art. 30(5): the obligations "shall not apply to an enterprise or an organisation employing fewer than 250 persons unless the processing it carries out is likely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, the processing is not occasional, or the processing includes special categories of data". A continuous hosted service is "not occasional", so the exemption is lost. DSK Kurzpapier Nr. 1 covers the records: https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/kurzpapiere.html **[read]** (list page; the paper itself not read).
- **[read]** Art. 33(2): "The processor shall notify the controller without undue delay after becoming aware of a personal data breach." Art. 33(1) gives the controller 72 hours to the authority, so the AVV should set a short notice time (a number is the lawyer's call). The operator needs an incident log and a stand-in for absences.
- **[read]** Art. 82(2): a processor "shall be liable for the damage caused by processing only where it has not complied with obligations of this Regulation specifically directed to processors or where it has acted outside or contrary to lawful instructions of the controller". Art. 83(4): fines up to EUR 10 000 000 or 2 % of worldwide turnover for infringements of "Articles 8, 11, 25 to 39 and 42 and 43" (this covers Arts. 28, 30, 32, 33, 37).

## 3. Security, DPO and key custody

- **[read]** Art. 32(1): "the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, including inter alia as appropriate: (a) the pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data". A leak would expose children's schedules, schools, possible sick notes and Source credentials that open the parents' mailboxes.
- **DPO. Corrected from the first version.** **[read]** Art. 37(1) covers public bodies, large-scale regular and systematic monitoring, and large-scale special-category processing as a core activity. **[read]** § 38(1) BDSG: a DPO is needed where at least 20 persons are constantly engaged in automated processing, and "Nehmen der Verantwortliche oder der Auftragsverarbeiter Verarbeitungen vor, die einer Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung nach Artikel 35 ... unterliegen, ... haben sie unabhängig von der Anzahl der mit der Verarbeitung beschäftigten Personen" a DPO. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bdsg_2018/__38.html . So a one-person operator needs a DPO (external is possible) if its processing is subject to a DPIA. Whether it is: see section 9.2, where the DSK Muss-Liste and WP248 are read and applied (a DPIA is probably indicated). DSK Kurzpapier Nr. 12 covers DPOs of processors **[read]** (list page only).
- **Key custody and ADR-0006.** The master key is a file in its own volume, and backups exclude it. When the operator runs the machine, the operator can read the key and the blobs, so encryption protects against dump leaks and a stolen backup, not against the operator. ADR-0004's statement that backup storage "stores only ciphertext" holds only if the backup key stays out of the operator's reach, which conflicts with operating restores for a non-technical Family. The contract and privacy notice must not promise that the operator cannot read Family data unless the architecture makes that true. **[unverified]** UWG §§ 5 and 5a on misleading statements were not re-read. Operator support access is itself processing: log and restrict it.

## 4. Special points

### 4.1 Children's data

- **[read]** Art. 8(1): the age rule applies "in relation to the offer of information society services directly to a child" where consent is the basis. The Admin is a parent, so Art. 8 is probably not triggered; lawyer to confirm, especially if Agenda Links are ever opened by children.
- **[read]** Recital 38: "Children merit specific protection with regard to their personal data". Recital 75 names the processing of data of "vulnerable natural persons, in particular of children" as a risk factor **[read]** (located by text search; the surrounding recital was not read in full).
- **[read]** Art. 9 special categories (health in sick notes, religion) are dealt with in section 9.1: the processor derives its basis from the controller (EDPB 07/2020 para. 80); what applies when the household controller is exempt is open. (The first version left this unverified.)

### 4.2 Third parties' data inside mails

- **[read]** Art. 14(5)(b): the information duty does not apply where "the provision of such information proves impossible or would involve a disproportionate effort". Art. 14 is the controller's duty. A Family inside the household exemption owes no notice; the operator as processor owes none directly. If the operator were a controller for some processing (1.2), the question reappears for every teacher or parent named in a mail. Lawyer question.
- Operating line: no use of message content beyond running the service (Art. 28(10)); no training by the LLM provider (contract and provider setting).
- Data-subject requests from third parties go to the controller. Under AUDITOR (1.2) the processor's duty to assist has no anchor for a household customer, so the AVV should say what the operator does anyway.

## 5. Retention and deletion on cancellation

- **[read]** Art. 28(3)(g) as quoted above: the controller chooses between deletion and return, and copies are deleted "unless Union or Member State law requires storage".
- **[read]** § 327p(3) BGB: the operator must, on the consumer's request, make content (other than personal data) available "unentgeltlich, ohne Behinderung durch den Unternehmer, innerhalb einer angemessenen Frist und in einem gängigen und maschinenlesbaren Format". § 327p(2): the operator may not keep using such content after the contract ends (with exceptions). https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__327p.html (**corrected:** the first version cited § 327q for this; § 327q concerns the effect of the consumer's data-protection declarations on the contract.)
- **Backups.** Art. 28(3)(g) gives no grace period for copies; state a fixed rotation window as part of the security measures rather than promise instant deletion. **[unverified]** no authority text on backup retention was found.
- **Sequence to design:** termination effective, read-only export window, deletion of the Instance, volumes and key, backups gone after the next rotation. Under the Data Act (6.4) the export window may need to be at least 30 days.
- **Operator's own records.** **[read]** § 14b(1) UStG: invoices "acht Jahre aufzubewahren", from the end of the calendar year of issue. **[read]** § 147(3) AO: books, inventories and annual accounts ten years, accounting vouchers (Buchungsbelege) eight years, other tax-relevant documents six years. https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ustg_1980/__14b.html , https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ao_1977/__147.html (**corrected:** the first version said the ten-year invoice period was "shortened to eight from 2025 (check)"; the current text gives eight years for vouchers and invoices and keeps ten for books and annual accounts.) These records are the operator's controller data, kept separately from Family data. **[unverified]** Art. 17(3)(b) was not re-read.

### 6.3 Withdrawal, withdrawal function, and cancellation

- **[read]** § 312g(1) and § 355(1), (2) BGB: distance contracts carry a withdrawal right; "Die Widerrufsfrist beträgt 14 Tage. Sie beginnt mit Vertragsschluss, soweit nichts anderes bestimmt ist." https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__312g.html , https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__355.html .

### 6.4 EU Data Act: switching and export

**[read]** Regulation (EU) 2023/2854, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/2854/oj (Official Journal text):
- Art. 50: "It shall apply from 12 September 2025." The chapter on switching (Chapter VI, Arts. 23 to 31) has no later date; Art. 50's later dates concern other chapters.
- Art. 2: a "data processing service" is "a digital service that is provided to a customer and that enables ubiquitous and on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable, scalable and elastic computing resources"; a "customer" is "a natural or legal person that has entered into a contractual relationship with a provider of data processing services"; "exportable data" is "the input and output data, including metadata, directly or indirectly generated, or cogenerated, by the customer's use of the data processing service", excluding IP- or trade-secret-protected assets.
- Art. 25(2): the contract must allow switching or porting "not after the mandatory maximum transitional period of 30 calendar days", set a maximum notice period "which shall not exceed two months", specify exportable data exhaustively, give "a minimum period for data retrieval of at least 30 calendar days" and "guarantee full erasure" afterwards.
- Art. 29: "From 12 January 2027, providers ... shall not impose any switching charges"; until then only reduced charges not above the provider's direct costs.
- Art. 30(5): where no common specifications exist, the provider shall "export all exportable data in a structured, commonly used and machine-readable format".
- Art. 31(1) and (2): the only carve-outs are services custom-built for one customer and test versions. **No exemption for micro or small providers appears in these articles.**
- **Open for the lawyer:** whether one managed single-tenant application Instance is a "data processing service" at all (the definition speaks of a shared pool of elastic computing resources, which better fits infrastructure and platform services). If it is, the export promise becomes a legal contract term, and consumers count as customers.

### 6.6 Merchant of Record (Paddle terms)

### 9.1 Special categories (Art. 9): does the operator need its own condition?

- **[read]** Art. 9(1) GDPR: "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs ... and the processing of ... data concerning health ... shall be prohibited." Der Auftragsverarbeiter braucht dann keine eigene Rechtsgrundlage zur Verarbeitung." ### 9.2 DPIA (Art. 35), the DSK list, WP248, and the DPO consequence

- **[read]** Art. 35(1): "Where a type of processing in particular using new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall, prior to the processing, carry out an assessment". Art. 35(3)(b): required for "processing on a large scale of special categories of data referred to in Article 9(1)". Recital 91 ends: "The processing of personal data should not be considered to be on a large scale if the processing concerns personal data from patients or clients by an individual physician, other health care professional or lawyer."
- **[read] The test.** Guidelines on DPIA, WP 248 rev.01 (last revised and adopted 4 October 2017, endorsed by the EDPB), https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/just/document.cfm?doc_id=47711 : nine criteria; "In most cases, a data controller can consider that a processing meeting two criteria would require a DPIA to be carried out." Applied here:
  - sensitive or highly personal data: met. The criterion "may also include data such as personal documents, emails, diaries" and covers data linked "to household and private activities (such as electronic communications whose confidentiality should be protected)", and special categories are in the mails;
  - vulnerable data subjects: met. "Vulnerable data subjects may include children (they can be considered as not able to knowingly and thoughtfully oppose or consent to the processing of their data)";
  - innovative use of new technology: probably met (a generative model reading mail), as "the use of a new technology, defined in 'accordance with the achieved state of technological knowledge' (recital 91), can trigger the need to carry out a DPIA";
  - large scale: unclear. WP248 gives four factors (number of data subjects, volume of data, duration, geographic extent). A few Families' mailboxes per Instance, but all Families together under one operator, is the lawyer's call; the individual-physician sentence in Recital 91 does not clearly cover a software provider.
  - matching datasets, scoring, systematic monitoring, automated decisions with legal effect, preventing a right: not met.
  So two criteria are met on the plain reading, which in WP248's words "in most cases" requires a DPIA.
- **[read] The DSK Muss-Liste** (list of processing for which a DPIA is required, version 1.1 of 17 October 2018, https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/ah/20181017_ah_DSK_DSFA_Muss-Liste_Version_1.1_Deutsch.pdf ): 17 entries; none describes extraction of dates from private mail. Nearest are no. 11 (AI "zur Steuerung der Interaktion mit den Betroffenen oder zur Bewertung persönlicher Aspekte", not met by extracting events) and no. 17 (Art. 9 data used to determine performance, not met). Note 1: "Diese Liste ist nicht abschließend". The list predates LLMs, and each Land also publishes its own list, which was not read **[unverified]**. So the list does not itself require a DPIA here, and WP248 points the other way.
- **Whose duty.** **[read]** Art. 35(1) names the controller; WP248: "The controller is responsible for ensuring that the DPIA is carried out ... but the controller remains ultimately accountable" and "If the processing is wholly or partly performed by a data processor, the processor should assist the controller in carrying out the DPIA and provide any necessary information (in line with Article 28(3)(f))." WP248 also notes that a product provider's DPIA can inform the deploying controller's: "the data controller deploying the product remains obliged to carry out its own DPIA ..., but this can be informed by a DPIA prepared by the product provider". For a **household** controller, no Art. 35 duty can bind the Family (1.2, AUDITOR: no attachment for Art. 35 and 36), so the realistic holder is the operator, as the one who designs the processing. Whether § 38(1) BDSG (below) treats a processing for an exempt household as one that "unterliegt" Art. 35 is **not settled by any source found**.
- **[read] DPO consequence.** § 38(1) sentence 2 BDSG: "Nehmen der Verantwortliche oder der Auftragsverarbeiter Verarbeitungen vor, die einer Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung nach Artikel 35 ... unterliegen, ... haben sie unabhängig von der Anzahl der mit der Verarbeitung beschäftigten Personen eine Datenschutzbeauftragte oder einen Datenschutzbeauftragten zu benennen." https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bdsg_2018/__38.html . So if the DPIA duty applies, the operator, a processor, must designate a DPO although it has no employees. § 38(2) BDSG makes the special dismissal protection of § 6(4) apply only when designation is mandatory.
- **What a one-person operator can do.** **[read]** Art. 37(6): the DPO "may be a staff member of the controller or processor, or fulfil the tasks on the basis of a service contract"; Art. 37(5): chosen "on the basis of professional qualities and, in particular, expert knowledge of data protection law and practices"; Art. 37(7): contact details are published and sent to the supervisory authority; Art. 38(6): other tasks must "not result in a conflict of interests", so the owner, who decides the purposes, is not a sound choice for DPO (WP 243 on DPOs was not read **[unverified]**). Options: an external DPO under a service contract; or first obtain the lawyer's written view that no DPIA duty attaches. The cost class is a retainer or per-hour engagement with an external DPO plus the one-off cost of a DPIA; **no figure is given because none was found in a primary source: ask for quotes.** A completed DPIA, kept as a product DPIA and shared with Families as WP248 describes, is the evidence either way.

### 9.4 AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)

One short paragraph, text read from the Official Journal (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj): **Not high-risk, on the text read.** High-risk status comes from Art. 6(1) with Annex I or from Annex III (Art. 6(2)); the Annex III heading closest to a school mail is education, which covers systems to "determine access or admission", to "evaluate learning outcomes", to assess "the appropriate level of education" or to monitor "prohibited behaviour of students during tests". The operator that wraps a third-party model into Family Agenda and sells it is probably a **provider of an AI system** (of the Family Agenda extraction function) and a **deployer** of the third-party model; each Family using it privately is outside "deployer". The model's own developer carries the general-purpose model obligations (Chapter V). Art. 4 (AI literacy of "staff and other persons dealing with the operation") applies to providers and deployers and, per Art. 113, Chapters I and II apply from 2 February 2025 and the rest of the Regulation, including Art. 50, from 2 August 2026, with Art. 6(1) from 2 August 2027. **[unverified]** Whether any amendment (the Commission's digital omnibus proposals) has changed these dates was not checked. ### 9.5 Fernmeldegeheimnis and Strafrecht for reading a mailbox with the Family's credentials

- **[read]** § 3(2) TDDDG obliges to secrecy "Anbieter von öffentlich zugänglichen Telekommunikationsdiensten sowie natürliche und juristische Personen, die an der Erbringung solcher Dienste mitwirken" and providers of commercially offered telecommunications services (https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/ttdsg/__3.html). A "Telekommunikationsdienst" is a service "in der Regel gegen Entgelt über Telekommunikationsnetze erbracht", namely Internet access, interpersonal communication services and services consisting of signal transmission (§ 3 Nr. 61 TKG, https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/tkg_2021/__3.html). The operator transmits no mail; it reads mail already delivered to the Family's own mailbox, at the Family's request. On the text, it is **not** a duty-holder under § 3(2), and § 206 StGB (breach of postal or telecommunications secrecy) applies to owners and employees of a business that provides such services (https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__206.html). **[read]** The Federal Constitutional Court held that the protection of Art. 10 GG for a mail on the provider's server ends "in dem Moment, in dem die E-Mail beim Empfänger angekommen und der Übertragungsvorgang beendet ist" for content stored in the recipient's own sphere, but protects "der zugangsgesicherte Kommunikationsinhalt in einem E-Mail-Postfach, auf das der Nutzer nur über eine Internetverbindung zugreifen kann" (2 BvR 902/06, order of 16 June 2009, https://www.bundesverfassungsgericht.de/SharedDocs/Entscheidungen/DE/2009/06/rs20090616_2bvr090206.html ). That protects against the State and shapes the **mail provider's** secrecy duty; it does not by itself make a service the account holder instructs a secrecy duty-holder. Confidence: medium-high; whether a hosting operator can "mitwirken" at the mailbox provider's service is a lawyer question.
- **[read]** § 202a(1) StGB: "Wer unbefugt sich oder einem anderen Zugang zu Daten, die nicht für ihn bestimmt und die gegen unberechtigten Zugang besonders gesichert sind, unter Überwindung der Zugangssicherung verschafft". The element is "unbefugt": access with credentials the account holder gives the operator for exactly this purpose is authorised by the holder. The risk is a **mailbox whose holder did not agree**: a partner's, a school-issued, an employer's or a shared mailbox for which the Admin supplies the password without the holder's consent. § 202b (interception of non-public data transmission by technical means) is aimed at tapping transmissions, not at reading a mailbox by IMAP. § 202c concerns preparing § 202a or § 202b by producing or distributing passwords or tools, so storing a password for authorised use is not within it (https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__202a.html, __202b.html, __202c.html). No court decision on a hosted mail reader was read **[unverified]**.
- **Wording that avoids doubt (for the sign-up and the Source form):** the Admin confirms that they are the holder of the mailbox or are authorised by the holder (and, for a shared or partner's mailbox, that the holder agreed); that they authorise the operator to log in and read the allow-listed folders and nothing else; that credentials are used only for that; and that they will not connect an employer's or a school's mailbox without that organisation's permission.
- **Mail providers' terms.** **[read]** Google: "An app password is a 16-digit passcode that gives a less secure app or device permission to access your Google Account" and "App passwords aren't recommended and are unnecessary in most cases ... use 'Sign in with Google'" (https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185833). **[read]** Microsoft: "We removed the ability to use Basic authentication in Exchange Online for ... POP, IMAP ..." (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/exchange/clients-and-mobile-in-exchange-online/deprecation-of-basic-authentication-exchange-online). So for Gmail and Microsoft the credential model in the Source form is an app password or OAuth, not the main password; the product should say which, and the notice should ask the Admin to use an app password or OAuth where offered. Each provider's terms on third-party access and automated use were not read **[unverified]**.

### 9.6 Security of LLM processing (Art. 32): prompt injection through a received mail

- **[read]** Art. 32(1) as above: measures "appropriate to the risk". **[read]** OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, LLM01 Prompt Injection (https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/): "Indirect prompt injections occur when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files." The impact depends on "the agency with which the model is architected"; "it is unclear if there are fool-proof methods of prevention", so the listed measures "mitigate the impact": constrain model behaviour; "Define and validate expected output formats"; input and output filtering; "Enforce privilege control and least privilege access"; human approval for high-risk actions; "Segregate and identify external content"; adversarial testing. **[read]** BSI, "Generative AI Models: Opportunities and Risks for Industry and Authorities", version 2.0 of 17 January 2025, R28: attackers can hide instructions "in websites, emails, or documents evaluated by the LLM (e.g., insertion of additional textual information like Unicode tags, which are processed but not displayed to readers)", with harm "assuming sufficient privileges and possibilities for action" (https://www.bsi.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/EN/BSI/KI/Generative_AI_Models.pdf). The DSK guidance on technical and organisational measures for AI systems (version 1.0, June 2025) was searched and has no passage on prompt injection **[read]**; ENISA was not read **[unverified]**.
- **What the pipeline already does** (code read; the research note `docs/research/llm-item-extraction.md` has no section on injection): no tools, URL fetching or actions, so there is no exfiltration channel from the model (the BSI and OWASP exfiltration examples need rendering of external images, a callable function or an outgoing mail, none of which exists); strict schema output with a second check in code and `additionalProperties: false`; a fixed instruction and the message as delimited data; the verbatim-evidence rule; the allow-listed folders; the model's output is only ever stored as Items.
- **What it does not do, as read:** (1) The instruction text does not tell the model to ignore instructions inside the message, and the `<<<`/`>>>` delimiter can be forged by a mail that contains `>>>`. (2) The evidence check proves the quote exists in the mail, not that the mail is trustworthy, so a mail that says "Parents' evening on Friday 20 November, bring EUR 500" produces a forged Item that passes every check, and there is no review before an Item appears in an Agenda (ADR-0016); an Admin can only delete it. (3) Hidden text (Unicode tag characters, white-on-white text in a PDF) reaches the model unchanged. (4) No sender-trust signal is used. The realistic impact is a false or missing Item and a forged "bring" or deadline, plus wasted compute, not data leakage, because the model has no way to send data out. Confidence: high for the code facts, medium for the risk assessment.
- **What the AVV/TOMs annex can state truthfully:** the model has no tools and no network access of its own; output is schema-constrained and checked in code; messages are treated as untrusted data; forged content can create or hide Items but cannot reach other Sources or accounts; Items can be deleted by the Admin; content is deleted after the retention period; the sub-processor receives only the message text and sent date, not the sender or credentials. It should **not** claim that injection is prevented. Candidate hardening, supported by the OWASP list and not required by law: tell the model to treat the message as data, strip control and tag characters before sending, escape the delimiter, and show an Item's evidence quote to the Admin so a forged Item is easy to recognise.

### 9.7 What a Family must be told or agree to, and what the product should show

One list, for the sign-up terms and a notice on each Source form:
1. **Who does what.** The Family decides which mailboxes and folders are read and why; the operator runs the Instance on its instructions (processor), and does not use the content for its own purposes or for model training.
2. **Which data.** Mail text and PDF attachments (after OCR) of the allow-listed folders, including data of teachers, other parents and children, and possibly health data and religion; passwords or tokens for the mailbox, stored encrypted.
3. **Who receives it.** The hosting provider, backup storage and the LLM provider, with their location (EU), and the link to the current sub-processor list; the Admin can switch the LLM off for a Source or choose a local endpoint (the code's acknowledgement for a non-local endpoint exists already and should show this text).
4. **Explicit consent** of the Admin to processing the Family's own special-category data in mails (Art. 9(2)(a)), and a statement that the Family itself decides how to treat third parties' data in its mail; no claim that third parties agreed.
5. **Mailbox authority.** The Admin is the holder of, or authorised by the holder of, each connected mailbox (section 9.5), and uses an app password or OAuth where offered.
6. **Limits of the automation.** Items can be wrong, missing or forged by a crafted mail; check important dates; an Admin can delete an Item.
7. **Retention and deletion.** Message content is deleted when no Item needs it or after about 180 days; whole-Instance deletion and export on cancellation (section 5).
8. **Data-subject requests and incidents.** Whom third parties ask; the operator's notice time for a breach.
9. **AI.** That a machine-learning model reads the mail (Art. 50 does not require it here, but the Family should know, and Art. 4 AI literacy covers the operator's own staff).
The product should show: the consent text at sign-up and again when the first mail Source is added; a per-Source notice naming the folders read, the LLM destination and region and whether it is local; a link to the AVV, the sub-processor list and the DPIA summary if one exists; and a visible way to disable the LLM or delete a Source and its content.
